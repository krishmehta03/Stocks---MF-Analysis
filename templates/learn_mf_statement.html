How to read your mutual fund statement

Updated 17 November 2026 · 7 min read

A mutual fund statement is a list of every transaction in each of your folios, followed by a closing balance in units. To read one, find the fund and its plan, then follow the transactions in date order: each shows an amount, the NAV that day, and the units you received. Your current value is simply your closing units multiplied by the latest NAV.

Which statement do you have?

People usually mean one of three documents:

  • A consolidated account statement (CAS) covers all your mutual funds across fund houses in one document. It is generated from the records of the registrars (CAMS and KFintech) and can be requested through the MFCentral platform, and a version that also shows demat holdings is available from the depositories.
  • A fund house statement comes directly from one fund house and covers only its schemes.
  • A platform or app statement is generated by the app you invest through and may be laid out differently.

The layout varies, but the same building blocks appear in all of them. The examples below use a made-up fund.

Step 1: Find the folio and the scheme

Each fund house assigns you a folio number, which is your account with that fund house. Under it, you will see each scheme you hold, usually with its registrar code and ISIN, a unique identifier for that fund and plan. If you have invested through several routes, you may have more than one folio with the same fund house. That is normal, but it is worth knowing when you add up your holdings.

Step 2: Check the plan and the option

The scheme name usually includes two details that matter:

  • Direct or Regular. Direct plans are bought without a distributor and have lower costs. Regular plans include a distributor's commission. On many statements, a field next to the scheme shows an ARN code for a distributor or the word DIRECT.
  • Growth or IDCW. In a Growth option, gains stay in the fund and show up as a higher NAV. In an IDCW (income distribution cum capital withdrawal) option, the fund pays out part of the money periodically, which reduces the NAV.

Direct and Regular plans of the same fund have different NAVs, so make sure you record the right one.

Here is an example for a SIP of ₹10,000 a month into a fictional fund. The statement lists each transaction on its own line:

Reading across a row: on 5 January the fund took ₹10,000. A small stamp duty (0.005% at the time of writing) is shown as a separate line, and the remaining ₹9,999.50 was divided by that day's NAV of ₹50.00 to give 199.990 units. The unit balance keeps a running total. Notice that the same ₹10,000 bought fewer units in February, when the NAV was higher, and more in March, when it was lower. That is how a SIP averages out your cost over time.

The fund, NAVs and numbers above are invented for illustration. Your statement's layout and wording will differ.

Other transaction types you may see

  • Purchase. A one-time (lump sum) investment.
  • Redemption. You sold units. The amount is units sold multiplied by that day's NAV.
  • Switch in / switch out. Money moved from one scheme to another within the same fund house. It appears as a redemption in one scheme and a purchase in the other.
  • IDCW payout or reinvestment. A payout of money from an IDCW option, or new units bought with it.
  • STP / SWP. A systematic transfer or withdrawal plan, which repeats on a schedule like a SIP in reverse.

Step 4: Understand the closing balance and value

At the end of each scheme, you will see your closing unit balance and the NAV on the statement date. Multiply them to get the market value. Using the example above, if the NAV on 31 March 2025 is ₹51.20, then 595.938 units are worth about ₹30,512. Against ₹30,000 invested, that is a gain of roughly ₹512, or about 1.7%.

Many statements also show a cost value, which is the amount you invested in the scheme. Compare it with the market value to see your gain or loss. Be aware that the NAV on a statement can be a day or two old, so the value will not match today's price exactly.

What a statement does not tell you

  • Your annualised return. A statement gives you the value, but it does not calculate XIRR or CAGR. For a SIP, you need XIRR. See CAGR vs XIRR vs absolute return for which to use.
  • How the fund compares to a benchmark or to similar funds.
  • Your tax position. Capital gains on redemptions are usually provided in a separate capital gains statement, which you can request from the registrar or fund house.
  • Your total portfolio. A mutual fund statement does not include the direct stocks you hold with a broker.

A quick checklist when you read yours

  1. Confirm each scheme is the plan you intended: Direct or Regular, Growth or IDCW.
  2. Check that the unit balance matches what your app or portal shows.
  3. Look for transactions you do not recognise, such as an old SIP you meant to stop.
  4. Note any scheme that appears under more than one folio.
  5. Record units, purchase dates and amounts for each fund, so you can track returns properly later.
